Every people names its troubles after someone else

Syphilis was French to the English, Italian to the French, Spanish to the Dutch and Christian to the Ottomans. This is the full pattern: diseases, vermin, manners, vice, incomprehension, and the milder branch where a turkey is Indian and a Danish is Viennese.

Ten sentences

In one breath

The whole pattern, one thing at a time, in the long form of the syphilis sentence. Each sentence draws only on entries from the tables below.

  1. Syphilis, commonly known to the English as the French disease, though also known to the French as the Neapolitan disease, and to the Italians mostly as the French disease and sometimes as the Spanish, and to the Dutch as the Spanish pox, and to the Portuguese as the Castilian disease, and to the Russians as the Polish disease, and to the Poles as the German disease, and to the Japanese as the Chinese sore or the Portuguese disease, and to the Persians as the Turkish disease, and to the Turks as the Frankish disease, and to the Indians as the disease of the Franks, and to the Ottomans and Arabs as the Christian disease.

  2. The 1918 influenza, commonly known to the world as the Spanish flu, though known to the Spanish as the French flu or the Naples soldier, and to the Senegalese as the Brazilian flu, and to the Brazilians as the German flu, and to the Poles as the Bolshevik disease, and to German soldiers as Flanders fever.

  3. Leaving a party without saying goodbye, known to the English as taking French leave and to Americans as the Irish goodbye, though known to the French as slipping off English-style, and to the Italians, Poles, Russians, Czechs, Hungarians and Romanians as leaving the English way, and to the Spanish, Portuguese and Germans as leaving the French way, and to the Austrians as the Polish exit.

  4. Incomprehensible talk, known to the English, Portuguese and Swedes as Greek, though known to the Greeks, French, Dutch, Spanish, Poles, Russians, Hungarians, Romanians, Estonians, Latvians and Israelis as Chinese, and to the Italians as Arabic, and to the Finns as Hebrew, and to the Germans as Bohemian villages, and to the Czechs and Slovaks as Spanish villages, and to the Turks as a thing that leaves you French, and to the Chinese as heavenly script or ghost scribbles.

  5. The turkey, an American bird known to the English as the bird of Turkey, though known to the Turks, French, Russians, Poles and Israelis as the bird of India, and to the Dutch, Danes and Swedes as the bird of Calicut, and to the Portuguese as the bird of Peru, and to the Egyptians as the Roman rooster, and to the Levantines as the Abyssinian rooster, and to the Greeks as the French girl, and to the Scots Gaels as the French hen, and to the Cambodians as the French chicken, and to the Malays as the Dutch chicken.

  6. The cockroach, known to the English as the German cockroach, though known to the Germans as the Swabian, and to the Russians and Poles as the Prussian, and to the Czechs and some Germans as the Russian.

  7. The condom, known to the English as the French letter, though known to the French as the English cape, and to the Germans as the Parisian.

  8. Splitting the bill, known to the English as going Dutch, and to the Koreans and Japanese by the borrowed “Dutch pay,” though known to the Italians as paying Roman-style, and to Spanish speakers as paying American-style, and to the Turks as the German method.

  9. Sodomy, known to the English and French of the Renaissance as the Italian vice, though known to the Germans of the same era as Florencing, and to the English, through the word bugger, as a Bulgarian habit, and to the French of 1900 as the German vice, and to newspaper classifieds as Greek.

  10. The Danish pastry, known to the English as Danish, though known to the Danes as Viennese bread, and to the Viennese as the Copenhagener.

Sentences 2 and 6 include usages tagged reported in the tables (the Senegalese, Brazilian, Polish and German-army flu names; the German-dialect “Russians” for cockroaches).

The pattern

Three moves, one reflex

When something unwelcome arrives — a disease, a pest, a bad habit, a sound you can’t parse — the name usually records who the speaker already distrusted, not where the thing came from.

Contagion

It came from over there

Syphilis, the 1918 flu, cholera, cockroaches, rats. The name tracks the war or the trade route of the moment, which is why the same disease gets a different villain in every language.

Manners

They behave like that

Leaving without goodbye, splitting the bill, drinking for courage, paying for sex. Rudeness and vice are exported to the rival next door.

Incomprehension

They talk like that

“It’s Greek to me” and its cousins form a directed chain that mostly ends at Chinese. Older still: whole peoples named as babblers or mutes.

Your second example belongs to a fourth, milder branch: names that record the last port a thing passed through. The turkey is the canonical case — an American bird that English credits to Turkey, Turkish credits to India, and Portuguese credits to Peru. There is no grievance in most of these, only a garbled supply chain. The last section covers the modern counter-movement, in which the World Health Organization now bans place names in new disease names.

Most-blamed

Counted across every entry below; an entry blaming two places counts for each.

Contagion

The Great Pox

Syphilis reached Europe in 1494–95 and within a decade nearly every people had named it after an enemy or a trading partner. The names map the politics of 1500 more accurately than the epidemiology.

Contagion

The 1918 influenza

Spain took the blame because, as a wartime neutral, its newspapers were free to report the outbreak. Inside Spain, the flu was French — or a song.

Contagion

Other diseases

Some are pure blame, some are honest geography that hardened into stigma, and a few (the English sweat, the English malady) were applied by the English to themselves.

Contagion

Vermin

The cockroach is always from the next province over.

Manners

Leaving without a word

Europe splits almost cleanly: English says French, and most of the Continent says English. German hedges with France and Poland.

Incomprehension

It’s Greek to me

Charted as a graph, most arrows converge on Chinese; Chinese points to heaven and ghosts.

Manners

Anglo-Dutch, and who pays the bill

Most of the English “Dutch” idioms date to the commercial wars of the seventeenth century, when “Dutch” became shorthand for cheap, drunk, or fake. Other languages assign the split bill to someone else.

Manners

Sex and vice

Sexual practices, contraception and even menstruation get the same foreign attribution as disease. The condom is the cleanest case: French to the English, English to the French.

Manners

Idioms of blame

Treachery, cheating, chaos, freeloading, playing dumb: each language keeps a neighbor on file for each failing.

Incomprehension

Names for outsiders

Before blaming a neighbor for a thing, languages often name the neighbor as the one who can’t speak properly.

The innocuous branch

Innocent misnomers

Your Dutch-auction example belongs here: names that record a trade route or a guess, not a grudge.

Renamings and reforms

Method

What’s in, what’s out

Included: any conventional expression that attributes a disease, pest, habit, object or quality to a people, place or language, whether hostile or merely mistaken. Excluded: plain ethnic slurs for people themselves (the nicknames for Germans, French, Italians and so on), since those name the group rather than assign it a thing, and one-off coinages without established usage.

The Dutch-auction example doesn’t check out. The Dutch name for a descending-price auction is veilen bij afslag (the Aalsmeer flower auction is the classic case); there is no established Dutch usage of “Chinese auction.” “Chinese auction” is an American term for something different — a raffle-style event where bidders buy tickets for chances at prizes. The English name is a straightforward misnomer-by-origin, which puts it in the innocuous branch, but the return volley from Holland appears to be folklore.
On the syphilis list. The core of the passage is sound. Two refinements: Italian sources mostly said mal francese, with “Spanish disease” the minority usage; and the Japanese name most often cited is tōgasa, “Chinese sore,” with “Portuguese disease” secondary.
